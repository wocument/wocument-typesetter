# Business glossy voice

*Measured:* 20 words per sentence on average; many numbers and figures, few contractions (formal), rarely says "we", rarely asks questions; American spelling.
Some pages repeat lines or split letters where the PDF layered its text, so this reading relies on the passages that came through cleanly.

## In short
This voice talks to ambitious readers who care about wealth, founders and deals: who made the money, how, and what it is worth now. It sounds upbeat and punchy, admiring with a raised eyebrow. Numbers are the plot. Every story gives a person, a price tag and a lesson for the reader's own business or portfolio.

## How a story opens
The commonest opening is a character sketch built on one vivid personal detail (clothes, accent, home, habit) before the money arrives. Example: "Odette Lambert wears a flour-dusted apron to board meetings. The 58-year-old founder of a 400-shop bakery chain says it reminds her where the money comes from: about $2 billion of it, by the magazine's estimate."

The second is then-and-now: a moment when the subject was doubted, then a jump forward to their present scale. Example: "When Marta Kovac opened her frame shop in 2009, suppliers told her the bicycle trade was finished. Sixteen years later her factory ships 900,000 frames a year."

Short front-of-book items lead with a striking figure followed by a colon: "Nine million spokes: That is how many the plant laced last year." First paragraphs are short, two to four sentences, and the dollar figure appears by the second or third paragraph.

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are one to three words in title case and usually pun on the subject's trade: "Rising Dough," "Spoke Person." The deck is the workhorse: one or two sentences that name the subject in capital letters, say what the business does, give the dollar figure and add a twist. Example: "For two decades MARTA KOVAC built frames for other brands. Now electric bikes have turned her contract factory into a $900 million business, and made her rich." A section label sits above, and lists get explicit ranking headlines such as "The Best-Paid Bakers of 2026."

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences average 20 words, mixing medium ones with quick follow-ups. Dashes are used more than in the other business voices, often to land a twist. Parentheses hold asides with figures ("(revenue: $12 million)"), and colons set up a payoff. Semicolons are rare; contractions turn up mainly inside quotes. There is no "I"; the publication says "we" only for its own estimates. Paragraphs hold two to five sentences.

## People and quotes
People are introduced with their age in the description: "the 52-year-old former welder". Quotes come every two or three paragraphs, chosen for personality, especially about ambition, risk or money. Attribution is present-tense "says" after the quote, or "tells" plus the publication's name. Outside comment comes from analysts and sometimes a social-media post. The writer never appears.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Dollar figures are everywhere (net worth, stake, valuation, revenue), rounded to one decimal ($7.2 billion), shortened in lists ($82 mil), with growth as a percentage year over year. The publication's own estimates are presented as authoritative. Technical ideas get one plain sentence and a concrete comparison. Readers are assumed to know investing basics such as market value.

## Words
American, energetic, confident: money words (fortune, windfall, stake, haul), sports and gambling metaphors, verbs from the subject's own trade. No academic hedging or vulgarity.

## Signature moves
- A price tag on every person within the first few paragraphs: "Her 60% share of the bakery chain is valued at roughly $1.1 billion."
- A pun on the subject's trade in a verb: "The welder forged a fortune."
- A twist in the deck, flagged by "yet" or "ironically".
- A sidebar caption with a two-word title and a short anecdote about the subject.
- A quote followed by a one-line wink: "Spoken like a baker."

## Endings
Features end on the subject's own words about what comes next, often followed by a short wry sentence from the writer, or look forward in money terms ("sales could double by 2028"). Stock-tip boxes end on a price target, a time frame and the author's credential.

## Never in this voice
- Vague claims without a figure.
- Long abstract analysis or heavy hedging.
- "I" or British spelling.
- A sneering takedown tone; skepticism stays light.
- Question headlines.
- A profile that leaves out the subject's age or fortune.
