# Founder's notebook voice

*Measured:* 20 words per sentence on average; talks to the reader as "you", often says "we"; American spelling.
The source text has captions and pull quotes breaking into the body in places; these notes rest on the clean passages.

## In short
This voice talks to founders and owners of small and midsize companies, people who read a story and ask, "What would I do?" It sounds like a well-connected peer: warm, direct, practical and sometimes funny. Every piece tries to hand the reader something usable: a lesson, a warning or a rule of thumb. The high "you" count comes from columns and advice pieces; long profiles stay mostly in the third person.

## How a story opens
Openings reach the point within two paragraphs. First paragraphs run four to six sentences, fewer in columns.
- **A personal mistake.** "When I opened my first bakery, a supplier told me to buy a second oven before I had a second customer. I did. It sat cold for a year."
- **A blunt truth to you, then a question.** "Your shop is only as good as its last delivery. But fuel is expensive and riders are scarce. So how do you keep promises without losing money?" Then: "To find out, we asked three owners."
- **A dated scene with something riding on it.** "It is a wet morning in March, and the bicycle factory's new frame line has twelve hours to pass inspection."
Interviews open with a quick backstory: the unlikely founder, one key number, the deal or crisis.

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are plain promises or claims: "How to Price a Cake Without Losing Your Regulars", "The Bridge Contract I Should Have Turned Down", "Stop Chasing Big Clients". Decks (the one or two sentences under the headline that say what the story is about) state the benefit or the lesson, often speaking to "you". A recurring column name sits above as the kicker (the small tag that names the slot). Wordplay is light and friendly, mostly in photo captions ("ROLLING ON").

## Sentences and rhythm
Middle-length sentences with plenty of contractions, rhetorical questions and colons that set up a payoff. Short fragments add punch: "No rent. No debt. More time." Dashes are common; semicolons almost never appear. Paragraphs are short. "I" is frequent in columns and as-told-to pieces (a founder's own words, shaped by a writer), "we" stands for the team or the magazine, and "you" arrives whenever advice is given.

## People and quotes
People get full name, title and a plain description of the company: "Maya Lund, founder of a cargo-bike repair shop". Age or a place on a growth ranking adds texture. "Says" in the present tense is standard, with "recalls", "explains" or "argues" for variety. Formats vary: interviews with bold questions, roundtables with speaker names in capitals, numbered lessons in the founder's voice. Columns carry a short author bio as the writer's credential.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers prove a lesson rather than decorate it: revenue, money raised, growth rates, headcount, the cost of a mistake. "Percent" is usually spelled out. Mechanics and rules are explained in plain steps, often with a concrete example in parentheses. Sources are named casually ("according to a recent survey of 500 owners").

## Words
Startup vocabulary (bootstrapped, exit, margins, runway) mixes with everyday idiom and the odd mild swear word. It prefers verbs of doing (build, hire, fix, ship) and concrete nouns. It avoids academic words and sweeping claims with no person behind them.

## Signature moves
- **The lesson as an order:** "Raise your prices before you need to."
- **The turn to the reader:** "If you sell anything perishable, this is your problem too."
- **The mistake and the recovery:** "We lost the contract in May and won it back by October."
- **The rule of thumb:** "Never sign a lease longer than your cash will last."
- **The aside with a grin:** "(Yes, the mascot was a pretzel.)"

## Endings
Advice pieces end on a principle stated plainly: "Grow slowly, and the bakery stays yours." Interviews close on the founder's next goal. Long profiles end with a wry line that turns an opening detail into a verdict, or a quote about legacy, followed by a tail byline (a closing line naming the writer and their role).

## Never in this voice
- Detached analysis with no takeaway for the reader.
- Long, semicolon-heavy sentences or academic vocabulary.
- Luxury gloss or grief; the mood is practical optimism.
- Advice with no real founder's experience behind it.
- Unexplained finance or legal jargon.
- British spelling.
