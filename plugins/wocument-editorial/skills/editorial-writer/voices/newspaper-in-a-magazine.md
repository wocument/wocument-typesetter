# Newspaper in a magazine voice

*Measured:* 23 words per sentence on average; many difficult words, rarely says "we", a wide vocabulary, few contractions (formal); British spelling.
The source text has missing spaces and broken letter pairs in places, so this reading relies on the passages that came through cleanly.

## In short
This voice talks to educated, globally minded readers who want the argument as well as the news. Stories are unsigned, so it speaks as one institution: dry, confident, well read and quietly funny. It explains how something works in as few words as possible, then says what it thinks. The reader should finish knowing the facts, the mechanism and the verdict.

## How a story opens
The commonest opening is an allusion: a historical episode, a line from a novel or an old anecdote, set against the present with a touch of irony. Example: "When the first cast-iron bridge opened in the 18th century, its builder died in debt. The council now planning its successor hopes to avoid the precedent."

The second is a crisp, counterintuitive statement, followed in the next paragraph by a turn on "But" or "Yet". Example: "Bicycle sales have doubled in five years. Bicycle factories are closing anyway."

Columns and obituaries open on a vivid character sketch: "He was proud of his ovens, and prouder of the bread." First paragraphs run 50 to 90 words; opinion pieces give the situation first and the verdict second.

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are very short, two to four words, usually a pun, idiom or cultural reference bent to the subject, and they often do not reveal the topic. The rubric (a one-sentence deck under the headline) does the explaining in a plain statement, often with a wry second half. Example: headline "Wheels within wheels"; rubric "A subsidy for bicycle makers is mostly helping carmakers." A section label sits above. Long pieces use small punning subheads to break the text.

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences are long and balanced, with subordinate clauses, then cut by a short verdict. Semicolons balance opposites; dashes carry asides; parentheses hold definitions and cross-references to other sections; colons introduce the explanation. Paragraphs hold three to six sentences. There is no "I". A columnist refers to themselves by the column's name, in the third person. Contractions are rare.

## People and quotes
Every person and organisation gets a compact gloss on first mention: "Jan Novak, a bridge engineer"; "Brightwheel, a bicycle-maker." Later references use "Mr" or "Ms" with no full stop. Quotes are fewer than in the other business voices and usually short fragments woven into a sentence. Attribution varies: says, argues, reckons, notes, insists. Many sources are described by role rather than name, such as "a senior official".

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are dense and abbreviated: $39bn, 1m, 5%. Dates take a suffix (November 13th), and imperial units get a metric conversion in parentheses. Sources get a one-phrase gloss. Technical terms are defined in brackets straight after use: "a kiln (an industrial oven)". The reader is assumed to follow world affairs, so famous people and institutions are named without fuss, but each mechanism is walked through with concrete numbers or an analogy.

## Words
Formal, British spelling (-ise, -our, programme), a wide, often Latinate vocabulary livened by the odd casual word (dodgy, boffin) for contrast. It likes "firm", "boss" and "reckon". It never uses hype words (game-changer, impactful).

## Signature moves
- The deflating short sentence: "The ministry promised a bridge a year. It has built two in a decade."
- Balanced antithesis with a semicolon: "Steel is cheap; welders are not."
- A gloss after every name, however brief.
- An old anecdote set against today: "Medieval millers were fined for short weight; modern bakers are fined for long labels."
- A plain verdict: "This is a mistake."

## Endings
Pieces end on a dry final sentence: an understated irony, a conditional look ahead ("If the subsidy lasts, the factories may not") or a resonant fact. Sometimes a short quote is followed by a few flat words of the writer's own. Opinion pieces end on a recommendation. A solid end mark closes every story.

## Never in this voice
- A byline voice, "I", or chatty "you".
- Exclamation marks or breathless adjectives.
- American spelling.
- Long quotes doing the explaining.
- Explaining what the reader already knows, such as who a head of government is.
- Question headlines.
