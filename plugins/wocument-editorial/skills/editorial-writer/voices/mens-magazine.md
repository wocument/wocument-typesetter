# Men's magazine voice

*Measured:* 17 words per sentence on average; asks many questions, short, plain words, a narrower, repeated vocabulary, a lot of "I" (first person, personal); American spelling.
The text was extracted with some run-together words and spaced-out running heads; the passages relied on here read cleanly. Many of the questions and much of the "I" come from question-and-answer pages and from reporters who put themselves in the story.

## In short
This voice talks to style-minded men as a knowing friend who has done the legwork. It is dry, confident and a little irreverent, and it can swing from a long reported profile to a two-line shopping tip without changing its grin. It wants the reader to feel in on the joke and ahead of the crowd.

## How a story opens
The first words are set in capitals and run into the sentence. Feature first paragraphs are long, eight to twelve lines; short items get to the point in one sentence.
- **Place first, person second.** A location with a little history, then the subject walks into it: "On a ridge above the river there is a bridge that everyone in town calls the Old Ferry, though no ferry has run since 1950. Last spring, an engineer climbed it at dawn."
- **The reporter's stunt.** "On the morning I tried to build my own bicycle, I spent an hour staring at the box."
- **The deadpan timestamp.** Exact time and date, flat delivery, a hint that this is the latest in a series: "The bakery's third oven fire began at 4:12 a.m. on a Tuesday."
- **The wry generalization.** "Few things make a cyclist happier than a stranger's flat tire."

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are short and sound spoken: a question to the subject ("Still Hungry, Chef?"), a quip, or a plain label for service. The deck (the sentences under the headline that say what the story is about) is longer, sets up the stakes, names the writer in capitals with a travel verb ("TOM HALE heads to the coast to…"), and often ends with a question. Credits follow on one line: by, photographs by, styled by.

## Sentences and rhythm
Plenty of short, punchy sentences between long reported ones. Closed em dashes, colons that set up a reveal, and parentheses for deadpan asides ("(He did not.)"). A question is often answered in brackets: "Was it raining? (Yes.)" Long pieces break into numbered sections, timestamps or spaced asterisks. The reporter's "I" is everywhere: I visited, he told me.

## People and quotes
Full name plus a credential clause, surname only afterwards. Quotes are frequent and long, in double quotation marks. Long profiles use past tense ("he said", "she told me"); short items use "says". The attribution usually lands after the first sentence of a quote. Question-and-answer pages print bold questions and mark answers with the speaker's initials. When someone will not talk, the piece says so plainly.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are dense: prices in dollars, ages, exact times, scores, heights spelled the way people say them ("six two"), "percent" as a word. Technical ideas are explained through a quick comparison from sport or film rather than a definition. Outlets and job titles are named; legal care shows in "is said to" and "appears".

## Words
American spelling, casual and sometimes profane, with style and sport slang (fit, grail, vibe). It repeats its key nouns rather than reaching for synonyms. Mock-formal words turn up for comedy, set beside blunt ones. No therapy language, no gushing adjectives.

## Signature moves
- Mock-epic framing of something ordinary: "the Great Toaster Collapse of last winter".
- Direct second-person advice in service copy: "You don't need a garage to fix a chain."
- Numbered product notes separated by short dashes.
- A dry parenthetical that undercuts the sentence before it: "The oven is state of the art (it caught fire twice)."
- A spoken line as headline, answered in the deck.

## Endings
Long features often jump to the back of the issue and end on a scene with a small ironic detail, or a quote that lands a joke. Short items close with the writer's name after a dash. First-person essays end with a one-line author biography.

## Never in this voice
- British spelling or single quotation marks.
- Exclamation marks used for enthusiasm.
- Earnest self-help language or sermons.
- Flowery description without a joke or a fact.
- A reporter who stays invisible in a long profile.
