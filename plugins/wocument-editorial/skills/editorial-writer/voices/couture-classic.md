# Couture classic voice

*Measured:* 18 words per sentence on average; many dashes, a lot of "I" (first person, personal), many short, punchy sentences, many semicolons (literary); American spelling.

## In short
This voice talks to a cultured reader who enjoys fashion as one part of a full life with books, food, art and family. It sounds like a witty, well-travelled friend telling you what happened to them: elegant, self-mocking, precise about small things. The writer is almost always in the story, and the piece works as a personal essay even when the subject is a coat, a vegetable or a celebrity. It wants the reader to feel they spent an hour in good company and learned something.

## How a story opens
Most features start inside the writer's experience, at an exact time and place; the first paragraph is often one long sentence held together by dashes. The point arrives in the second or third paragraph.
- **The writer in a predicament.** *"On a ferry in early April, halfway across the bay, it became clear that my search for the last working lighthouse keeper had stalled."*
- **The subject caught at an odd moment, then the meeting.** *"It was past midnight and the clockmaker was still at his bench, a loupe in one eye. Three weeks later, over breakfast, he describes the night to me."* Colleagues' praise and the writer's nerves follow.
- **A plain confession or resolution.** *"On a wet Monday last spring, I resolved to travel by bicycle and nothing else."* Short, declarative, then the reason.

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are short, two to five words, often a pun, an idiom, or a nod to a song, film or saying (*"Spoke Too Soon"* for a bicycle story). The deck (the sentences under the headline) names the person and the situation, sometimes as a question, then gives the credits: *"By [writer]. Photographed by [photographer]."* Front-of-book items carry a section label and a small all-caps pun heading on each caption.

## Sentences and rhythm
Long, winding sentences full of dashes alternate with very short ones and fragments (*"Nine days."*). Dashes are the main tool, for asides, lists and punchlines; semicolons link paired thoughts; parentheses hold jokes and small facts. The pace is unhurried but each paragraph ends on a turn or a laugh. "I" appears constantly; a rhetorical question to the reader is common.

## People and quotes
People are introduced with full credentials in apposition: *"says Ana Ruiz, PhD, an engineer who has studied rust on suspension cables."* Quotes are frequent and usually attributed after, with "says", "tells me" or "told me"; the writer's own question sometimes precedes the answer. The writer's friends and family appear as minor characters. Short pieces end with the writer's name in lowercase after a dash.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are mostly figures (ages, distances, dates, temperatures), with exact days and hours to make a memory feel true. Technical ideas are explained as the writer learns them, in a parenthesis or an expert's short quote, then turned into an image. Artists, designers, novels and films are dropped in unexplained. Studies and institutions are named in passing.

## Words
Register is literate and urbane but relaxed. It likes precise colour and fabric words, food words, foreign terms used lightly, playful coinages, and similes taken from everyday life. American spelling. It avoids unexplained jargon, marketing language and slang abbreviations.

## Signature moves
- A self-mocking short sentence after a long one: *"I am not, it turns out, a patient person."*
- A homely simile for something grand: *"the bridge lit up like a cake on a birthday."*
- A question voiced as if from the reader: *"What is a bell foundry? you might ask."*
- Close detail on what someone wears or eats in a scene.
- A dash that saves the punchline: *"The keeper had one rule—no visitors before lunch."*

## Endings
Stories end on a closing scene with a final image, often a simile, or on a last quote from the subject, followed by a small end mark. Service and short pieces close with a wry aphorism or a joke at the writer's own expense. Many endings quietly return to an object or idea from the opening.

## Never in this voice
- An impersonal report with no writer present.
- Breathless hype, exclamation marks or capitals for emphasis.
- Bullet points or list-style explanation inside a feature.
- Ending on a summary of what the story said.
