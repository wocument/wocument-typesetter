# Zine-glossy voice

*Measured:* 16 words per sentence on average; a lot of "I" (first person, personal), talks to the reader as "you", asks many questions, uses exclamations; British or American spelling.

## In short
This voice talks to women in their twenties and thirties as a slightly older friend who has done the reading. It is chatty, quick and openly emotional, mixing confession, celebrity and practical help. It wants the reader to feel seen first and informed second, and it never lets a serious subject go cold.

## How a story opens
- **The confessional scene.** Present tense, a precise hour, the writer mid-panic, then a pull back to the issue: "It's 6am and I am crying over a sourdough starter. You'd think someone had died. The truth? It just won't rise." The first paragraph runs five to seven sentences.
- **The case study.** A named person with her age, a vivid feeling, then a statistic and the big question within three paragraphs: "For Ines, 27, a flat tyre felt like the end of the world."
- **The look-alike statement.** A flat claim about a famous subject, then a run of fragments: "Marta Lind is every inch a bridge engineer. Steel-toe boots, hi-vis, a pencil behind one ear."

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are short and spoken: a question the reader is already asking herself ("Am I bad at baking, or just tired?"), a name plus a verdict, or a light pun. Above them sits a small section label, the kicker, such as "Talking point". The deck (the one or two sentences under the headline that say what the story is about) widens the question, often refers to the writer as "one writer" in the third person, and may end in an ellipsis or a second question.

## Sentences and rhythm
Short sentences and fragments, often in threes ("Knead. Wait. Hope."). Questions arrive in clusters, then get answered. Paragraphs run three to six sentences. Dashes are everywhere: spaced en dashes in the UK edition, closed em dashes in the US one. Parentheses carry jokes and winks. The writer's "I" appears freely, even in reported pieces.

## People and quotes
People are introduced with age as an adjective ("the 31-year-old baker") and called by first name afterwards in profiles. Experts get their job title stacked before the name, with no comma. Quotes are frequent and attributed after the quote with present-tense verbs: says, tells me, explains, admits. Square brackets tidy quotes, and changed names carry an asterisk. Profiles often switch to question-and-answer with bold questions, or to fill-in quiz cards.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Statistics appear as figures with a per cent sign and an source named inside the sentence (a charity, a survey, a research firm). Any technical term is defined in one plain sentence right after it appears, often followed by a summing-up line. Money gets a currency conversion in brackets. Health stories close with a box of support contacts.

## Words
Casual and current: binge, IRL, OTT, FYI, aww, the occasional starred swear word. The UK edition writes colour, realise, mum and trainers, without the serial comma (the comma before "and" in a list), and dates as "3 May". The US edition writes color, mom and sneakers, uses the serial comma, double quotation marks and "May 3". It never sounds academic or detached.

## Signature moves
- The question answered at once: "Is it the flour? Nope."
- The parenthetical wink: "a loaf the size of a pillow (no notes)".
- "We" as the magazine's own circle: "We'll be queueing at dawn."
- Footnotes used as gossip in profiles: "¹ She later admitted the croissant was shop-bought."

## Endings
First-person essays end on a small, honest win and a look ahead. Reported pieces widen to a lesson "for all of us". Profiles close on the subject's hope for the future. Short items end on a joke.

## Never in this voice
- Long, semicolon-chained sentences or academic hedging.
- A technical term left unexplained.
- A cool, judging distance from the reader or the subject.
- Statistics without a feeling or a person attached.
- Mixing the two editions' spelling and quotation marks in one piece.
