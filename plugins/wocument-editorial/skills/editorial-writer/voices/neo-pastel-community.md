# Neo-pastel community voice

*Measured:* 21 words per sentence on average; few short sentences, rarely asks questions, often says "we", almost no exclamations; American spelling.

## In short
This voice writes for people who make, rent, fix and share homes, and who care how a place came about as much as how it looks. It sounds friendly and curious, like a well-read neighbor, and puts people before objects. It wants the reader to see a home as a set of decisions, limits and relationships, and to leave with an idea they could use.

## How a story opens
The opening goes straight to a person: within the first paragraph (50 to 90 words) the reader learns who, where and what limit they faced.
- **The person's background:** "Before she designed bridges, the engineer spent three summers welding bicycle frames in her uncle's garage. You can see it in her joints."
- **A quick chain of events:** "Two weeks after the bakery's lease ran out, its owners found a shuttered bank down the street and signed for it the same afternoon."
- **A reporter's scene, in the first person:** "On the factory's top floor, past the paint booth, I find the oldest frame builder eating lunch beside a stack of forks."

## Headlines and decks
A headline is a short, familiar phrase or a pairing of opposites ("Loud Quiet", "Small Change"), set in lower case or caps as the design asks. A deck (the one or two sentences under a headline that say what the story is about) is a full sentence with a full stop. It gives place, person and twist, in everyday words: "On a corner lot in a river town, two bakers turn an old bank into a shop with a reading room upstairs." Sometimes the headline runs into the deck on one line. Every story carries a section label (a "kicker") such as "RENTERS" or "COST SHEET", and credits are given as TEXT BY and PHOTOS BY.

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences are long but easy to follow; the length comes from clauses joined with "and", "but" and "while", and from asides in em dashes. Semicolons and colons are rare. Paragraphs run three to six sentences. The editor's letter and list introductions speak as "we" (the editors) and sometimes as "I". A reported piece may let the writer appear in the first person.

## People and quotes
People are introduced by role, then name: "furniture maker Jo Park". After that, the story often uses their **first name**, especially in household stories: "Jo explains". Quotes are frequent, long and conversational. The attribution usually comes after the quote: "says" in the present tense, and also "explains", "recalls", "adds" and "notes". Some formats hand the story over to the residents. A diary piece alternates speakers under small subheads.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
The practical numbers appear early: floor area, number of units, how long the build took, ages and costs. Budget stories add an itemized table and a total. Floor plans come with a short fact panel (designer, location). Terms are explained inline at once: "a granny flat, or small backyard unit". The voice assumes a curious reader, not an expert.

## Words
The register is warm and plain, and sometimes casual. It uses everyday words for materials and building (plywood, lot, permit, siding, shed). Social words come up often: neighbors, block, friends, gathering, community. Now and then it uses a light slang word or a coined word for warmth. Spelling is American. It avoids grand adjectives and words about status.

## Signature moves
- It names the limit and treats it as the engine of the design: "With one budget and no crane, they built the roof in sections on the lawn."
- It turns to the residents' wider circle: "On Saturdays the workshop doubles as a repair café for the street."
- Captions act as a second narrator, keyed with "opposite" and "below": "The owners (opposite) share the stair with their tenant (below)."
- It states a belief plainly and then moves on: "Good houses are argued over. This one was."

## Endings
Stories end on a resident's reflective quote about values, belonging or how the place changed them, or on a short reflection from the writer that returns to the opening or looks ahead.

## Never in this voice
- Luxury language, designer-name parades or price-tag glamour.
- Distance or irony toward the people featured.
- Exclamation marks, or questions used as hooks.
- Leaving a technical or cultural term unexplained.
- Hiding the money, the setbacks or who did the work.
