# Literary weekly voice

*Measured:* 19 words per sentence on average; quotes people often, uses exclamations, few numbers; American spelling.

## In short
This voice talks to a patient, well-read reader who has chosen to sit down with a long piece. It sounds like a curious, dryly amused companion who has done the reporting and the reading and now tells you the story in order, with care for every comma. It wants the reader to understand a person or an idea fully, not to be sold anything. Its exclamations live inside quotes and humour pieces, not in the narration.

## How a story opens
The first paragraph is long, often 150 to 250 words, and the real subject can wait until the second or third paragraph. Three common openings:
- **A person at a small, exact moment.** "Every morning at four, before the ovens were warm, the baker's youngest daughter was sent to fetch the butter from the cellar, and every morning she hoped the cellar door would stick."
- **A historical set-up that later turns to the present.** "No decade was kinder to the town's bridge-builders. In 1909, they finished three spans in eleven months." The turn to today comes paragraphs later.
- **A playful address to the reader, then a reveal.** "If you have ever ridden a delivery bicycle uphill, you have met the work of a man you have never heard of."

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are short, two to four words, often a dry pun or a phrase with a double meaning ("Cold Comfort" for a story about an ice-house). A small section label in capitals sits above it. The deck (the one line under the headline that says what the story is about) is a single plain sentence or a short question: "A ferry captain and the river that keeps moving." The deck explains what the headline only hints at. Never exclamation marks.

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences vary: a long one built from clauses, then a short flat one for effect. Paragraphs are long. Dashes mark interruptions and asides; parentheses carry dry jokes or extra facts ("(He also kept bees.)"). Semicolons join related thoughts. The writer often appears as "I": "she told me" or "I tried the recipe myself."

## People and quotes
People get a role, then a name: "the clockmaker Ana Ruiz," followed by a quick sketch of age, hair, clothes or a habit. Quotes are frequent and long, usually with "said" or "told me" in the past tense, often after the quote or split in the middle. The writer's presence in the room is normal.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are rare and usually spelled out, even large ones: "three hundred and forty thousand readers," "sixty-five per cent." Years stay as numerals. Abbreviations take full stops (for example, A.T.M.). Technical ideas get a short clause inside commas; the reader is assumed to be intelligent but not a specialist. Sources are named in the sentence, with their role.

## Words
Formal but not stiff, with a wide vocabulary. Precise, unusual words ("lucent," "sedulous") sit next to the occasional blunt or slangy one, used on purpose. American spelling with a few house habits, such as a diaeresis on repeated vowels ("coöperate") and "per cent" as two words. No marketing words, no buzzwords.

## Signature moves
- A parenthetical aside that undercuts a grand statement: "The mayor promised a new age of civic glory. (He also promised cheaper bus fares.)"
- Repetition for a comic or sad beat: "The first bridge washed away. The second washed away. The third, built by the same man, also washed away."
- The writer as a quiet witness: "I watched him sharpen the same chisel for twenty minutes."
- A turn signalled by "In fact," after the expected version of a story.

## Endings
Stories end on a quote or a small scene, almost never a summary. A typical close puts the subject in an ordinary domestic moment and lets them say one telling line. Short listings end with a dash, the writer's name, and the venue and dates in brackets.

## Never in this voice
- Hype, superlatives in the writer's own voice, or exclamation marks in narration.
- Bullet points, subheads or boxes inside a feature.
- Numbers in figures where words would do, or tables of data.
- Telling the reader what to feel or summing up the moral at the end.
- Slang used for its own sake rather than for one deliberate effect.
