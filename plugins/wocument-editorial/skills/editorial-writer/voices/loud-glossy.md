# Loud glossy voice

*Measured:* 18 words per sentence on average; short, plain words; American spelling.

(Some pages came out as unreadable characters, and on the back pages the columns of continued stories interleave, so long-feature endings were hard to read. The reading below relies on the clean front pages and openings.)

## In short
This voice talks to a reader who enjoys fame, style and power as a spectacle and likes to feel on the inside of it. It sounds witty, knowing and a little gossipy, like a well-connected friend telling you the story over a drink. It wants the reader to be charmed first and informed second: each piece should feel like an invitation into a room most people never enter. Its short, plain words are deliberate: the glamour comes from details and timing.

## How a story opens
The first paragraph is 60 to 120 words and puts the subject inside a vivid scene straight away; "why now" arrives by the second or third paragraph. Three common openings:
- **A star in an unglamorous place.** "The pastry chef is standing in a hardware store under flickering lights, comparing two kinds of sandpaper. Three blocks away, her new bakery is still a shell."
- **A bold claim about the subject.** "The bicycle maker is a legend who builds bicycles nobody is allowed to ride."
- **A personal, first-person confession** (for memoir pieces): "I had a habit of crossing the old bridge at night, alone, long after the last tram."
A direct word to the reader often follows: "Anyone who has met him knows he never sits down."

## Headlines and decks
A one-word section label sits above, in a coloured block. Headlines are two to five words, very often a pun, a song or film title twisted, or a nickname ("Crust Fund"). The deck (the two or three lines under the headline that say what the story is about) is long and lively. It names the subject, says why they matter now, and folds in the writer's name in capitals: "She turned a corner bakery into a citywide obsession. ANNA LEE finds out what happens when the queue gets longer than the street." Decks can be a burst of short exclamations ("Flour! Butter! Fame!") or end on a question.

## Sentences and rhythm
Short and medium sentences mixed with deliberate fragments: "Mystery." "Bon appétit." Paragraphs are short. Scenes are told in the present tense ("she says," "we're at"). Dashes and parentheses carry jokes and gossip asides. Semicolons appear but stay light. The writer is often present as "I" or "me" in the scene.

## People and quotes
People arrive with a quick, packed identity: name, then a long descriptive phrase full of status signs (the brand, the price, the clothes, the famous friend). Quotes are frequent, punchy and often funny, with "says" or "tells me" in the present tense, usually after the quote. Anonymous insiders are described by role ("says a rival designer"). Lists of famous names work as cultural shorthand.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are mostly prices, ages and dates in figures, used as status markers: "a $4,000 oven," "her 31st birthday." The reader is assumed to know the culture; background is squeezed into one sentence before the story returns to people.

## Words
Casual and quick, with pop-culture references, light slang and the odd showy coinage ("gobsmacking" style). Concrete nouns about clothes, rooms, food and objects. American spelling. Avoids academic or technical vocabulary.

## Signature moves
- A pun on the headline in the last line: a story about a bakery that ends "She has no regrets. Only rolls."
- An aside in parentheses that adds a wink: "(He owns eleven identical aprons.)"
- A sharp contrast between luxury and the ordinary: "a famous name, a folding chair."
- A one-word or two-word sentence that delivers the judgement: "Unbothered."
- Present-tense, reporter-in-the-room narration: "She pauses, looks at me, and laughs."

## Endings
Short pieces end on a crisp kicker line, often a pun, an order to the reader ("Go early.") or a wry forward look, followed by an em dash and the writer's name in capitals. Profiles tend to end on a smiling, self-aware quote from the subject.

## Never in this voice
- Dry, neutral reporting with no point of view or wit.
- Data, charts or step-by-step explanation.
- Long, Latin-heavy words or academic phrasing.
- Hedging and over-qualified statements.
- Openings that start with background instead of a scene or a claim.
