# Illustrated research journal voice

*Measured:* 19 words per sentence on average; often says "we"; American spelling.

## In short
This voice talks to curious, educated readers who are not specialists: people who want to know what a new technology really does, who is building it, and whether it will work. It sounds like a well-informed friend who has read the studies and visited the lab, warm but quietly skeptical. It explains clearly and leaves the reader with a fair verdict rather than excitement. The frequent "we" comes from editor's letters and package introductions ("we asked", "we called"), and from "we" meaning society as a whole.

## How a story opens
- **A person in a dated moment.** A named person, a year, a place and one detail. "In 2019, a baker in a small river town noticed that her sourdough rose an hour faster every summer."
- **A plain claim, then the turn.** What has always been true, then "But". "Bridges have always been inspected by people with clipboards and binoculars. But a few engineers now let the steel report on itself."
- **A long setup cut short.** A long buildup, broken by a paragraph of a few words. "For a century, only one mill in the valley could weave this cloth. Last spring, a second one opened."

The first paragraph runs about 50 to 90 words. The paragraph that says why the story matters arrives by the third or fourth paragraph, often as an open question.

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are short (two to five words) and often a light pun, a play on a familiar phrase, or a question: "Rising to the occasion" for a bakery. The deck (the one or two sentences under the headline that say what the story is about) is plain and complete: it names the subject and the tension and ends with the byline. "A pedal-powered loom was meant to save the village mill. It worked, briefly." Recurring sections carry a small label above the headline (a kicker).

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences average about 19 words, but the rhythm comes from mixing long explanatory sentences with very short ones for emphasis ("It doesn't."). Paragraphs run three to six sentences. Dashes appear more than average, mostly to define a term inline or add a quick aside. A colon often sets up a payoff, followed by a capital letter. Parentheses carry dry asides. Writers use "I" when they report a scene in person and freely in columns.

## People and quotes
Full name first, then role and institution after a comma: "Ana Ruiz, a materials scientist at a state university, ..." Later references use the surname only. Quotes are frequent, short and spoken, chosen for life rather than information, and attributed with "says" in the present tense, after the quote or splitting it. Supporters and critics are both quoted.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are steady but a little less dense than average: digits from 10 up, "%" for percentages, money as "$38 million". Every big figure gets a familiar comparison: "about the size of four football fields," "enough to heat 2,000 homes for a winter." Studies are named by journal and month. Jargon is defined between dashes on first use. Hedges are honest: "so far," "it's unclear," "for now."

## Words
Middle register, clear and concrete, American spelling. It likes strong ordinary verbs (snag, rally, ferry), the occasional idiom, and about one pun per story. It avoids hype words (revolutionary, disruptive) unless questioning them, academic padding, and slang outside quotes.

## Signature moves
- **The promise, then the catch:** "The kiln cuts fuel use by half. But nobody has built one bigger than a garden shed."
- **The punch paragraph:** a long buildup, then "The ferry never sailed."
- **The writer's verdict:** "My money is on the cheaper kiln."
- **The skeptical tail:** a claim ends with "yet" or "for now."

## Endings
Features end on a quote from the main person looking ahead, a small ironic or tender scene, or a return to the opening image. Columns end on a clear judgement. Every piece closes with a one-line bio: name, role, home city.

## Never in this voice
- Breathless hype or press-release language.
- Scene-setting that still has no point by paragraph four.
- Unexplained jargon or acronyms.
- Swearing or snark in the writer's own voice.
- "Experts say" without a name and role.
