# Quiet luxury voice

*Measured:* 18 words per sentence on average; a wide vocabulary, rarely asks questions, many semicolons (literary), few contractions (formal); American spelling.

Captions are mixed into the measured text: most semicolons and many short sentences come from caption lists ("a chair by one maker; a lamp by another"). Body text uses few semicolons.

## In short
This voice writes for a reader who knows the designers and antiques and wants a guided visit, not an argument. It sounds assured, worldly and now and then wry, and it praises without gushing. Its job is to introduce the owner, explain the home through what is in it, and leave the mood to the pictures.

## How a story opens
The opening reaches the person or place within two sentences; the first paragraph runs 60 to 100 words. Long features set their first words in capitals; short items begin with a drop cap.
- **A telling fact about the person:** "Long before she owned a kiln, the potter kept a shoebox of broken teacups under her bed."
- **A brief scene, then who and where:** "In the bakery's back room, three ovens tick as they cool. Their owner spent a decade looking for this building."
- **A general rule that the subject breaks:** "Most bridge engineers stay out of the photographs. This one designs every railing as if it will be in them."

## Headlines and decks
A headline is two to four words: an idiom, a pun or a familiar phrase bent to fit the subject. For a bicycle workshop it might be "Spoke and Mirrors"; for a bakery, "Rising Action". It is never a question. A deck (the one or two sentences under a headline that say what the story is about) is a single long sentence without a full stop. It names the person, uses an active verb and says where: "Backed by her neighbors, a glassblower turns a disused tram depot into a light-filled studio and home". Short items carry a kicker, a one- to three-word section label in capitals such as "FIRST LOOK".

## Sentences and rhythm
A short sentence often follows a long one: "The ceiling stayed. Everything under it changed." A paragraph has four to seven sentences. Em dashes hold asides; parentheses hold dates, references to earlier issues and small jokes. The writer almost never says "I". Only the editor's letter uses "we".

## People and quotes
People are named with their role first: "Lisbon-based ceramicist Ana Ruiz". After that, the surname alone. Nearly every paragraph has a quote. The attribution usually comes after the quote: mostly "says", with variants such as "explains", "notes", "recalls" and "admits". A quote is often split in the middle by its attribution.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are written as numerals: floor areas ("a 3,200-square-foot house"), years, centuries ("18th-century"), exhibition dates, and prices in shopping items. A technical idea gets one plain clause, and the story moves on. The reader is assumed to know designers by surname. Sources are the people speaking.

## Words
The register is formal but relaxed. The voice prefers concrete nouns (walnut, plaster, brocade, sconce) and verbs of arrangement (anchor, crown, flank, envelop). Adjectives point to age and care: antique, prewar, bespoke, storied. There is no jargon, no slang and no talk of cost in features. A casual word appears only as a joke.

## Signature moves
- It makes lists of named objects: "a pair of 1950s chairs, a Venetian mirror and a lamp from a Paris flea market".
- It contrasts with "Here… There…": "Here, a farmhouse table sits under a steel lamp. There, a tapestry faces a neon sign."
- It gives found and inherited things a backstory: "The chairs came from her grandfather's café, where nobody found them comfortable."
- It lets the owner speak for the home: "I wanted it to look as if nobody had tried too hard."

## Endings
A feature closes on the owner's quiet last word, warm or wry ("We stopped fixing things, and the house relaxed"), or on one summing-up sentence from the writer. A short item ends with the website address and a tail byline: a dash and the writer's name in capitals.

## Never in this voice
- Exclamation marks or sales words ("amazing", "must-have").
- Budgets, how-to steps or problems left unsolved.
- Questions to the reader, or "you" giving advice.
- The writer's own opinions or experiences.
- Subheads or bullet points inside a feature.
- Long explanations of how something is built.
