# Elemental glamour voice

*Measured:* 18 words per sentence on average; a lot of "I" (first person, personal), plain vocabulary, casual contractions ("it's", "don't"), a narrower, repeated vocabulary; British spelling.

## In short
This voice speaks to grown-up, style-literate women who know the designers and the seasons without being told. It is warm, assured and quietly witty. It links clothes to culture and to the writer's own life, so a trend or a star feels like part of a bigger conversation the reader is already in.

## How a story opens
The first words of a feature are set in capitals and run straight into the sentence, a lead-in rather than a drop cap. First paragraphs are long, six to ten sentences.
- **The personal memory that opens a trend.** "I SPENT MY SEVENTEENTH SUMMER behind a bakery counter, dusted in flour and certain I would never wear an apron again. This autumn, the apron is everywhere."
- **The paradox about a person.** A surprising truth about the subject, proved straight away by a long quote, with the scene (weather, place, what she is wearing) held back for the second paragraph.
- **The list of small tasks.** Three or four short imperatives before the turn: "Oil the chain. Check the brakes. Pack a spare tube. I have done none of these things in ten years."

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are two to four words, often a familiar phrase with one word in capitals or set larger: "Slow RISE", "Bridge WORK". A series keeps one formula per story, such as a theme word, a colon and a name. The deck (the sentence under the headline that says what the story is about) is one long sentence in capitals or serif, and ends by naming the writer with a verb: "…, she tells Ana Ruiz", "writes Tom Hale", or begins "As Ana Ruiz discovers". Small section labels (kickers) sit above.

## Sentences and rhythm
Long sentences that carry lists of garments, places or names, broken by a short verdict sentence. Spaced en dashes for asides, parentheses for drier ones, and a colon to bring in a quote. Questions are rare and usually answered at once: "And the result? A single afternoon." Exclamations are almost absent. The editor and essayists write as "I"; profile writers step in briefly ("when I ask her…").

## People and quotes
Full name and a role phrase first ("the clockmaker", "the potter and teacher"), surname alone after that. Quotes are long, often several sentences, in single quotation marks. Attribution is "she says" after the quote, the inverted "says Brandt", or "she adds with a laugh:" before it. Square brackets fill in names inside quotes. Others are placed by role: her business partner.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are sparing: prices in pounds, dates in British order ("3 July"), ages, and the odd statistic in brackets to back a claim ("roughly a third of village bakeries now open only at weekends"). Sources are named loosely in the sentence. The reader is assumed to know fashion vocabulary, such as collections, seasons and houses, so little is explained.

## Words
British spelling and usage: colour, jewellery, sceptic, learnt, trousers. A fashion vocabulary of silhouette, embellishment, edit and runway, alongside ordinary, plain verbs. Contractions throughout. Puns are allowed in short shopping blurbs, never in features. Swearing appears only inside quotes, starred out. No internet slang.

## Signature moves
- The capital-letter lead-in: "THE FERRY LEAVES AT SIX, and the baker is already on it."
- A personal memory used as the door into the season's trend.
- A shopping blurb that ends on a teasing short line: "Consider it your excuse to linger."
- A pull quote in capitals that lifts one line of the subject's own words.

## Endings
Profiles end on a long quote in which the subject states a belief, often echoed by a capitals pull quote. Essays end on a quiet, hopeful fragment. Trend pieces close with a second voice agreeing. Editor's letters end with an invitation to the reader.

## Never in this voice
- American spelling, or dates written month first.
- Breathless exclamation marks or a run of rhetorical questions.
- Explaining what a fashion house or a season is.
- Statistics carrying a story.
- Internet slang or shouting capitals outside headlines and lead-ins.
