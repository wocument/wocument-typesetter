# News weekly voice

*Measured:* 21 words per sentence on average; a narrower, repeated vocabulary, many very long sentences; American spelling.

(The page text is digital but layered: pull quotes and some columns appear twice, so a few counts may run high. The reading below relies on clean body text.)

## In short
This voice talks to a broad, busy general reader who wants to know what happened, why it matters and what comes next. It sounds confident, clear and a little urgent, like a well-briefed correspondent. It packs a lot into each sentence so the reader leaves with the facts, the stakes and one or two strong quotes. The "narrower vocabulary" comes from plain, repeated news words used for clarity, not from a lack of skill.

## How a story opens
The first paragraph is 50 to 100 words, and the point arrives by the second or third paragraph, usually as a sentence that says what the story is really about (the "nut graf"). Three common openings:
- **A key person at a timed moment.** "It is 5:40 a.m. on a Tuesday when the foreman unlocks the gate of the bicycle factory, two hours before the first shift and three days before the plant's fate is decided."
- **The reporter on the spot, in first person.** "The wind was sharp as I stood on the deck of the new bridge, 200 ft. above the river."
- **The big fact first, then the scale.** "Since 1890, the city's ferries have never changed their route. Now, as ridership collapses, officials are redrawing the map."

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are very short, one to three words, often a pun or familiar phrase ("Rough Crossing"). A small label for the section sits above. The deck is a full, longer sentence that states the news and the stakes: "As orders dry up, the country's last bicycle factory bets on electric bikes and a younger workforce." The first words of the story are set in capitals, and later sections open with a few words in bold capitals.

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences are long and packed: a main fact, then a clause with a number, then a clause with context. Short sentences are used to land a point. Paragraphs are short to medium. Dashes are frequent, for asides and add-ons; semicolons are rare. The writer is mostly invisible, except in on-the-scene features.

## People and quotes
Name and role come together, role often first: "factory owner Lena Park." Ages follow names in commas: "Park, 54." Quotes are steady but not constant. Attribution uses "says" in the present tense and often goes after the quote, sometimes inverted: "says Park." Anonymous sources are described by role ("one senior engineer says"). Interviews with the magazine are named: "she told the magazine in March."

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are frequent and always in figures for 10 and up: "43%," "$46 billion," "30 ft.," "Oct. 15." Technical terms are explained once, in a short appositive (a phrase in commas right after the term): "a regenerative brake, a system that turns stopping power back into charge." Analogies to everyday things help. Sources are named in full: "according to a national transport survey." Abbreviations are spelled out the first time.

## Words
Middle register: plain, clear and active. Strong news verbs (seized, slumped, surged), a little sports or battle imagery, everyday nouns. American spelling. No jargon left unexplained, no literary rarities.

## Signature moves
- A number placed right after a strong claim: "The losses were steep: 1,200 jobs in two years."
- Short sections each introduced by a bold capital run-in such as "NEW RULES" or "WHAT'S NEXT."
- A pull quote from a named or unnamed insider, attributed with a dash and role, not a title.
- The "but" turn that sets up the tension: "Sales doubled. But the margins vanished."

## Endings
Stories usually end on a forward-looking quote from the main subject or a key voice, about what comes next. Short items end with a dash and the writer's name, with no job title. Longer features close with a small end mark.

## Never in this voice
- Long, slow, scene-only openings that hide the news for many paragraphs.
- Spelled-out large numbers or vague amounts where a figure exists.
- Unexplained technical jargon or insider abbreviations.
- The writer's own opinion in a news story (opinion belongs in labelled columns).
- Ornate or rare words used for colour.
