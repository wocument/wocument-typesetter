# Airy business monthly voice

*Measured:* 21 words per sentence on average; many difficult words, many very long sentences; American spelling.
The source text has columns and captions run together in places; these notes rest on the clean passages.

## In short
This voice talks to managers, investors and curious professionals who want to know where a company or industry is heading. It sounds composed and well informed, with a polished vocabulary and the odd flash of wit. Its job is to turn a business development into a story with stakes: who wins, who loses, and what a sensible leader should take from it. The long sentences come from the layered features; short front-of-book pieces move faster.

## How a story opens
Features take three to five paragraphs to reach the point, and the first paragraph runs three to six sentences. Common patterns:
- **A scene from the past that rhymes with now.** "In 1931, the owner of a lakeside ferry line threatened to move his boats to the rival town across the water. Within a week, the council had found the money for a new dock."
- **A time span that sets up change.** "In the nine years since she took over the family bicycle factory, almost everything her father relied on has stopped being true."
- **A wry riddle.** "Ask a room of investors which bakery chain grew fastest last year and most will point to the one with the neon sign. Hardly anyone mentions the wholesaler that mills its flour."
After the opening, a turn sentence, often starting with "But", states the bigger claim and what is at stake.

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are short statements or direct questions: "Can a Bridge Builder Learn to Write Software?" Decks (the line or two under the headline that says what the story is about) are longer. In one sentence they name the person or company, the achievement and the tension, then the byline. A one-word section label sits above as a kicker (a small tag naming the section). Wordplay is mild and mostly kept for photo captions, which open with a two-word label in capitals ("FLOUR POWER").

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences are long and layered, adding history, context and caveats, then a short sentence lands the point ("It worked."). Em dashes without spaces are frequent, for asides and swerves. Colons set up a reveal: "The message was plain: pay up, or lose the plant." Parentheses carry disclosures and updates. Semicolons are rare. Paragraphs run three to five sentences. The writer stays out of most stories but uses "I" and "me" naturally in interviews and first-hand product or lifestyle pieces.

## People and quotes
People appear by full name followed by role or credential: "says Ana Ruiz, an economist who studies small ports." Later mentions use the surname only. Quotes are frequent and often run two or three sentences; contractions appear mostly inside them. Experts get "says"; reported interviews get "said" or "told me". Anonymous sources come with a reason, and anyone who declined to respond is noted.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are dense: dollar amounts with "$" and "billion", percentages with the % sign, rankings, years and market values, often softened with "roughly" or "nearly". Sources are named inside the sentence ("according to the trade group"). The reader is assumed to know market value, earnings and stock listings; narrower ideas get a quick explanation in a clause or parenthesis.

## Words
Educated and confident, a notch above everyday speech. It likes vivid business verbs (wager, unwind, squeeze, rattle), weighty nouns (stakes, leverage, reckoning) and the occasional cheeky slang word to keep the tone human. It avoids unexplained jargon and chummy filler.

## Signature moves
- **The stakes sentence:** "At stake is who controls the flour supply for half the state."
- **The fair hedge:** "The mill was arguably the town's real employer."
- **Both sides weighed:** "Either side could walk away; neither can afford to."
- **The short punch after a long build:** "Then the orders stopped."
- **The parenthetical update:** "(The bakery has since reopened.)"

## Endings
Stories end on a forward-looking quote from the main figure, a measured verdict that both sides need each other, or a crisp two-sentence echo of the opening image: "The ferries are gone. The rivalry is not."

## Never in this voice
- Numbered lessons or how-to steps addressed to the reader.
- Cheerleading without tension or a counterpoint.
- Unsourced figures or a vague "experts say".
- Breathless, slang-heavy sentences or exclamation marks.
- A writer whose opinions dominate a profile.
- British spelling.
