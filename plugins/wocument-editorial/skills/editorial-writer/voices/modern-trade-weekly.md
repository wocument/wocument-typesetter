# Modern trade weekly voice

*Measured:* 22 words per sentence on average; quotes people often, many very long sentences; American spelling.

(Pull quotes and some captions appear twice in the page text, so the quote count may run a little high. The reading below relies on clean body text.)

## In short
This voice talks to industry insiders and close followers who want to know who is up, who is out and how much money moved. It sounds fast, sharp and well sourced, like a reporter who has just come off the phone with three insiders. It wants the reader to understand the deal, the power shift and the stakes in as few paragraphs as possible. Its reviews and lists switch to a warmer, opinionated first-person voice.

## How a story opens
The first paragraph is 50 to 110 words, and the business stakes arrive by the second paragraph. Three common openings:
- **The recent event, then the reversal.** "Last spring, the owner of the bicycle factory promised a bold five-year plan. Eighteen months later, the plan is gone, and so is she."
- **A question about a key person.** "What does a chef do in the twelve hours before the restaurant world learns she has won its top prize?"
- **A counter-intuitive statement.** "The era of the giant ferry has ended; the era of the giant ferry contract has not."

## Headlines and decks
News headlines are two or three words, very often a pun on a person's name, a title or an industry phrase ("Dough Boys"). The deck (the line under the headline that says what the story is about) is one sentence that begins with "Why," "How" or "Will" and gives the angle: "Why the city's two biggest bakeries stopped talking." Big features use a long deck in capitals that states the shift in power. A short section label and the date sit in a ruled header above.

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences are long and packed, built from a fact, a title, a sum and a clause of context. Paragraphs are short. Spaced dashes ( — ) are common for asides and add-ons; semicolons and parentheses appear often too, the parentheses usually holding credits or years ("(2019)"). News is told in the third person; reviews use "I" and "you."

## People and quotes
People are given a full job title, often before the name ("bakery co-owner Sam Ortiz") and sometimes after, in commas. Quotes are frequent and carry the story. Attribution uses "says" in the present tense, after the quote; past-tense "said" appears for statements and earnings calls. Anonymous sources are common and described by role ("says one rival executive," "sources suggest"). Companies "declined to comment," and statements go "to" the magazine by name.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are everywhere and always in figures: deal values, salaries, percentages, dates ("$3 million a season," "11.8%," "Oct. 6"). Titles of works go in quotation marks. Industry jargon (tentpole, spinoff, greenlight, slate) is used without explanation; the reader is assumed to be an insider. Occasionally a term gets a short, slightly wry gloss. Analysts and research firms are named with their roles.

## Words
Brisk, informed and slightly slangy. Business verbs (ousted, poached, clinched, slashed), industry shorthand ("biz," "nod"), and lively images of rise and fall. American spelling. Little ornament.

## Signature moves
- A pun headline built on a name: a baker named Bell gets "Saved by the Bell."
- The money right after the news: "The bakery sold for $14 million — about twice its asking price."
- An unnamed insider quote that captures the mood: "Everyone wants the job until they see the books," says one supplier.
- Lists and rankings with a short, opinionated paragraph per entry, signed with the critic's initials.
- Red-carpet items: date, venue, then a quip the guest "joked" to the reporter.

## Endings
News stories end on a pointed quote from an insider or analyst, often with a twist, or on a short forward look (the new owners are said to be quietly optimistic). Reviews end on a judgement. List entries end with a "must see" pointer and initials. Endings are quick and never summarise.

## Never in this voice
- Long, slow scene-setting before the stakes are clear.
- Claims without a source, or a deal without its price.
- Explaining basic industry terms at length.
- Literary flourishes, rare words or a dreamy tone in news pieces.
- Spelled-out large numbers.
