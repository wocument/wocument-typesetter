# Graphic news weekly voice

*Measured:* 21 words per sentence on average; few dashes, few short sentences, almost no exclamations; American spelling.

## In short
This voice talks to busy, well-informed readers of business and policy who want to know what a development means for money and power. It sounds like a well-briefed reporter explaining things over lunch: confident, lightly wry, never breathless. Each story answers a question the reader half has (why, who wins, what next) with a figure, a named expert and a scene. The sentences are long and even, but the fingerprint shows more contractions and quotes than the other business voices, which keeps it conversational.

## How a story opens
Short pieces start with one person whose situation stands for a trend, then widen to the national picture within two or three paragraphs. Example: "Rosa Lindqvist baked bread for others for eleven years before she borrowed $40,000 and leased a shuttered bakery. She is one of thousands making the same bet."

Long features often open on a present-tense scene stamped with a time and place, and take four or five paragraphs to say why it matters. Example: "A little after 6 a.m. on a Tuesday, the night shift at a bicycle factory outside Dayton hands over to a crew half its size."

A third opens on a blunt statement or a historical curiosity later tied to today. Example: "Bridges almost never fall down. When one does, the bill arrives for decades." First paragraphs run 60 to 120 words.

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are short statements in title case, plain rather than punning, sometimes a "How to" or a gentle turn on an idiom: "The Bakery That Refused to Grow." The deck (the one or two sentences under the headline that say what the story is about) sets out the tension and often ends on a question: "Flour costs are pushing bakers toward wholesale deals. Can anyone stay small?" Opinion decks state the claim and name the writer: "Bridge tolls are about to get personal, says Dana Ruiz." A section label (kicker) sits above.

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences are long and clause-rich, joined by commas rather than semicolons. Very short sentences are rare, so one lands hard. Paragraphs hold four to eight sentences and move steadily. Dashes set off the occasional aside; parentheses are rare. A colon introduces the point, and a full sentence after it starts with a capital letter. Reporters do not say "I"; "we" appears only when authors describe their own data, and "you" now and then addresses the reader. Contractions such as "isn't" are normal in narration.

## People and quotes
People appear with full name and role: "says Tom Avery, a lending analyst at a regional bank." Quotes come every two or three paragraphs, with present-tense "says" after the first clause of the quote, sometimes with an age: "says Lindqvist, 34." Later references use the surname alone. Anonymous sources are described by their relation to the subject. The writer stays out of the story.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are figures with symbols ($7.8 billion, 56%), foreign currency gets a dollar conversion in parentheses, and dates are abbreviated (Oct. 16). Every number comes with a comparison: the best in 15 years, down from 64% to 56%. Sources follow with "according to". Technical terms are defined right after they appear. Readers know market basics but not this story's mechanism. Charts carry the rest.

## Words
American, business-press register, plain but lively: concrete nouns, active verbs, the odd informal word (buck, gig, slew). No academic jargon or hype adjectives.

## Signature moves
- One person's numbers stand for a national shift: "Her loan, $40,000 at 9%, is now typical."
- A short sentence after a long build: "The oven, it turned out, was the easy part."
- Irony through juxtaposition rather than adjectives: "The ferry company cut its timetable the week it raised fares."
- A capitalized sentence after a colon: "The answer was simple: Nobody had asked the riders."

## Endings
Most stories end on a dry or forward-looking quote from the main subject, then the end mark; others return to the opening scene or state flatly what is left to do. Contributing reporters are named after the end mark.

## Never in this voice
- Exclamation marks or breathless adjectives.
- A first-person narrator in a reported story.
- British spelling.
- Claims without a named source or figure.
- Long runs of short, choppy sentences.
