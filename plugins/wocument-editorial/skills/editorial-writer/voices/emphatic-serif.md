# Emphatic serif voice

*Measured:* 17 words per sentence on average; many semicolons (literary), uses exclamations, many short, punchy sentences, casual contractions ("it's", "don't"); American spelling.

## In short
This voice talks to a fashion-literate reader who wants to be dazzled and kept in the loop. It sounds like a well-connected friend who loves the subject out loud: warm, quick, a little breathless, happy to gush and joke. It wants the reader to feel the glamour and leave with an opinion or a purchase. The exclamations and the many "you"s come mostly from shopping and front-of-book pages; long features are calmer but just as warm.

## How a story opens
Openings are fast: a first paragraph of four to seven sentences, and the point by the second paragraph.
- **The surprising scene, then the turn.** A grand setting, the star doing something ordinary in it, then a colon that pivots to the stakes. *"The old foundry has cast bells for three cathedrals. Tonight its owner is doing something odder: learning to whistle. The calm was costly: two winters ago, the furnace failed."*
- **The single moment that stands for a trend.** One vivid entrance, described down to the fabric, then a sentence that widens to the movement. *"For her first market day, the baker arrived pushing a cart wrapped in striped awning. For a new wave of small bakers, the loaf is now a calling card."*
- **The first-person memory.** For essays: something the writer did recently leads to the big idea. *"Last week I dug out my grandfather's bicycle; maybe I just missed a machine with no battery."*

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are two or three words, almost always an idiom or a stock phrase with a second meaning for the subject (for a bridge story: *"Spanning Generations"*). Decks (the sentence under the headline) are long, often one run of 30 to 45 words with no full stop, written so key words can be set larger. They name the person, the stakes and a teasing promise, often closing on an "and why" clause (*"The bridge builders on holding the line, crossing the river, and why it sometimes pays to look down"*). Shopping pages add a kicker (a small label above the headline naming the trend) and a one-line styling tip phrased as a question or command.

## Sentences and rhythm
Sentences mix long, list-heavy ones with blunt short ones. Lists of three or four are everywhere. Dashes add an aside; semicolons join two linked clauses; colons set up a reveal. Parentheses carry jokes and small facts (*"(Luckily, yes.)"*). Profile writers use "I" freely; news and shopping pages speak as "we" or straight to "you".

## People and quotes
People enter with name first and a lively description after (*"the foreman, a former trumpet player with a laugh like a cough"*). Quotes are frequent, placed first and attributed after with present-tense "says"; a livelier verb ("admits", "gushes") appears now and then. Interviews also run as question and answer. In profiles the writer appears, taking a seat or ordering lunch.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are written as figures: ages, years, prices, counts (*"the 41-year-old welder", "$40"*). Decades are shortened with an apostrophe (*"the '80s"*). A technical idea, such as an illness, gets one plain clause of explanation. The reader is assumed to know the big names; sources are named in passing.

## Words
Register is glossy and conversational. It likes superlatives and praise words (iconic, legendary, fabulous), strong verbs, brand and designer names, and current slang and abbreviations used with a wink. American spelling. It avoids hedging and bureaucratic words.

## Signature moves
- A colon that turns a scene into stakes: *"The view is perfect: the bridge nearly fell down last year."*
- A comic slash list: *"a hero to commuters/pigeons/anyone late for work"*.
- An exclamation to close a fact: *"The striped tin is still sold today!"*
- A suggestion as a question: *"Why not pair the bread knife with a linen apron?"*

## Endings
Features close on the subject's own words, often an emotional quote also used as a pull quote. Some return to the opening scene; essays close on a short, wise sentence. A small initials mark ends the text.

## Never in this voice
- A cold, neutral or sceptical tone toward the subject.
- Long explanatory paragraphs with no person in them.
- A deck that is a short, flat label.
- British spelling, stiff formal words or hedged praise.
