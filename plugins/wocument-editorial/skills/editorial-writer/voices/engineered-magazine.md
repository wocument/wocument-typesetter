# Engineered magazine voice

*Measured:* 19 words per sentence on average; close to the average on most measures; American spelling. (Measured from machine-read text, which turns many dashes into hyphens, so its dash count is too low and the other measures are rougher than for the other profiles.)

*Reading note:* the text is machine-read and noisy; this guide relies on passages that read cleanly.

## In short
This voice talks to readers who live online and know the platforms, the gadgets and the jargon, and who want the story told with attitude. It sounds quick, irreverent and sure of itself, like a sharp reporter who loves technology and distrusts the people selling it. It turns technology into stories about power and money: who wins, who gets hurt.

## How a story opens
- **Caps lead-in on an exact moment.** First words in capitals, a clock time, a scene turning odd. "AT 6:12 ON a Tuesday, the night baker discovered that the ovens had ordered their own flour."
- **The writer in the scene.** First person from the first line. "THE FACTORY MANAGER hands me a bicycle frame and tells me not to drop it."
- **A blunt claim and a short follow-up.** "BRIDGES ARE BORING. That is their job."
- **The detour.** An oddity first; the real subject appears later.

First paragraphs are short, often 30 to 60 words.

## Headlines and decks
Headlines are puns on films, songs, sayings or book titles, usually in capitals: "KNEAD FOR SPEED" for a bakery. The deck (the one or two sentences under the headline that say what the story is about) is punchy and ends on an exaggeration or a deadpan joke: "Lighthouses are now so cheap a village can afford three, and nobody knows who switches them off." Recurring series carry a kicker (a small label above the headline) and a fixed joking strap.

## Sentences and rhythm
The average sentence is ordinary, but there are more very short and very long sentences than in most profiles. Paragraphs are short, two to four sentences. Colons are everywhere, often followed by a capital. Dashes and parentheses carry wry asides: "(It did not.)" Semicolons are rare. A direct "you" is common. Writers say "I" when they test a product, report in person or argue an essay.

## People and quotes
People get a quick, vivid sketch along with name and role: "a soft-spoken ferry captain who keeps a tide table taped to his wrist." Quotes are frequent and raw; swearing stays in when it was said. Attribution is "says" after the quote. The magazine refers to itself by name in the third person. A favourite format is a one-person monologue in the speaker's own words, framed by a short introduction and an "As told to" credit.

## Facts, numbers and explanation
Numbers are dense and exact: clock times, dollar sums, and "percent" written as a word. A term is explained once in a quick clause ("a sourdough starter, a jar of live yeast that bakers feed like a pet") and then used without apology. There is no hand-holding. Sources are named people, court papers and company statements.

## Words
Casual and irreverent, close to speech. It likes slang, internet words, coinages, pop-culture references and hard verbs (trounce, bilk, brick). Spelling is American, even in pieces written in Britain, which keep pounds and local references. Swearing is fine in quotes and, sparingly, in the writer's voice. It avoids corporate words and nervous hedging.

## Signature moves
- **The deadpan parenthesis:** "The factory promises zero waste. (The dumpster outside is full.)"
- **The ironic last fact:** a final detail that undercuts everything before it.
- **The physical analogy:** a technical point made with toys, kitchens or cars.
- **The writer as test subject:** "I let the app run my bakery for a month."

## Endings
Stories end on a kicker: an ironic last fact, a quote with a twist, a one-line thought from the writer or a callback to the opening image. Then comes an end mark and a tail credit with the author's name in bold capitals and a short description.

## Never in this voice
- Neutral, distant reporting with no point of view.
- Long, semicolon-heavy sentences or academic phrasing.
- Advertorial warmth (the sponsored pages in the issues are not a model).
- Enthusiasm without a catch.
- Openings that take more than three paragraphs to reach the stakes.
